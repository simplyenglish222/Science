Topic 1 — AI assistance and authorship

Some people believe that writers and content creators who use artificial intelligence should still be regarded as the sole authors of their work, while others disagree. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Artificial intelligence has become increasingly integrated into writing and digital content production, raising questions about whether a creator who uses such tools can still claim sole authorship. While some argue that authorship should depend primarily on who develops the underlying ideas and takes responsibility for the final product, others contend that extensive reliance on machine-generated content makes such a claim difficult to justify. In my view, AI assistance is compatible with human authorship, provided that the technology does not replace a substantial part of the creative expression.

Those who defend AI-assisted creators point out that modern authors already rely on numerous tools. A writer may use spelling software, search engines, editors and generative AI to improve their work while retaining control over the structural blueprint, argument and final decisions. From this perspective, the decisive factor is not whether technology was involved but whether the human creator remained intellectually responsible for the work. AI can, for example, smooth over the prose or suggest alternative formulations without changing the writer's underlying ideas.

However, the situation becomes more problematic when creators outsource content to an algorithm. If substantial passages are generated by software, a distinction emerges between originating an idea and producing its literal expression. In such cases, an unqualified claim of sole authorship may mislead consumers who believe they are paying for human expertise. Moreover, audiences may reasonably expect creators to disclose the extent of AI use, particularly when creative authenticity is part of the product's appeal.

Overall, AI should be regarded as a legitimate creative tool rather than an automatic threat to authorship. Nevertheless, the greater the proportion of machine-generated content, the weaker the case for presenting the final work as entirely human. Clear disclosure can therefore preserve both technological efficiency and public trust.

As generative artificial intelligence becomes a routine part of content production, some argue that creators should be legally required to disclose its use. I largely agree with this proposal because consumers are entitled to know whether a substantial part of a product is machine-generated, although minor forms of automated assistance should not trigger the same requirement.

The strongest argument for disclosure is that the origin of content can materially affect consumer expectations. A reader who buys a specialist book may believe that its wording reflects the author's human expertise, while a subscriber may support a creator partly because of that person's distinctive voice. If a substantial amount of the final product consists of algorithmic output, concealing this fact risks creating a breach of trust. A clear declaration of the extent of AI use would allow consumers to make an informed choice without preventing creators from using new technology.

Mandatory disclosure could also strengthen professional standards. Publishers, media companies and online platforms increasingly act as professional gatekeepers, yet commercial pressure may encourage them to cut corners when automation reduces production costs. Requiring transparent labelling would make quality control easier to scrutinise and could protect institutional credibility. Importantly, a candid admission should not merely function as pre-emptive damage control; it should provide meaningful information about how AI contributed to the final work.

Nevertheless, regulation should distinguish between generative involvement and ordinary digital assistance. Requiring a formal warning whenever software corrects punctuation or suggests a synonym would be excessive and difficult to enforce. Disclosure should therefore apply when AI generates or substantially rewrites content rather than when it performs minor editorial functions.

In conclusion, significant AI involvement in paid creative work should be disclosed because it affects both consumer choice and trust. Sensible regulation, however, must focus on substantive generation rather than routine technological assistance.

The rapid growth of machine-generated content has complicated the question of who should be held responsible for poor or misleading material. Some believe that responsibility rests mainly with the individual creator, whereas others argue that publishers and platforms should share accountability. I believe that institutional responsibility should depend on the degree of editorial control an organisation exercises over the content.

There are reasonable grounds for placing primary responsibility on creators. Large digital platforms host enormous quantities of material and cannot realistically verify every sentence before publication. The individual who submits the work usually knows how it was produced and is therefore in the best position to disclose the use of generative AI. Excessive liability for platforms could also discourage innovation and lead companies to remove legitimate material simply to avoid legal risk.

Traditional publishers, however, occupy a different position. They select manuscripts, employ editors, market finished products and often present their involvement as a guarantee of quality. If an editorial team knowingly approves machine-generated content without adequate quality control, it becomes difficult to treat the organisation as a passive intermediary. Professional gatekeepers derive commercial value from their institutional credibility, and this creates a corresponding duty to maintain editorial standards. Otherwise, consumers may reasonably view the resulting failures as a breach of trust.

A balanced approach is therefore required. Open platforms should be expected to provide effective reporting systems and clear AI policies, but they should not automatically be liable for every piece of user-generated material. By contrast, organisations that actively edit, endorse and sell content should bear substantially greater responsibility for what reaches the public.

In conclusion, accountability should follow control. The more deeply a publisher or platform is involved in selecting and refining content, the stronger its obligation to ensure that automation is used transparently and responsibly.

Topic 4 — AI and creative quality

Generative artificial intelligence has dramatically reduced the time required to produce text, images and other media. Supporters claim that this technology expands creative possibilities, while critics fear that easy automation will fill the market with low-quality and repetitive work. In my view, AI can enhance creativity, but only when it supports rather than replaces the human creative process.

On the positive side, generative tools lower barriers to experimentation. A person with limited technical skills can rapidly test several visual concepts, restructure a draft or explore alternative ways of expressing an idea. This allows creators to devote more attention to underlying ideas instead of spending excessive time on routine production. AI may also help independent creators compete with larger organisations by reducing the arduous work of creating preliminary material.

The danger, however, is that efficiency can encourage people to cut corners. When creators rely heavily on AI, they may accept polished algorithmic output without developing a distinctive voice or checking whether the material is accurate and coherent. As machine-generated content becomes easier to produce at scale, audiences may face an enormous quantity of superficially competent but unoriginal work. In addition, creators who repeatedly outsource difficult tasks to an algorithm may fail to develop the skills that traditionally emerge from doing the hard yards themselves.

For this reason, the decisive issue is not whether AI is used but how it is used. Human judgement, revision and quality control remain essential. The strongest creators are likely to use automation to accelerate experimentation while retaining responsibility for the final expression and ensuring that their work reflects genuine expertise.

Overall, artificial intelligence can broaden creative opportunities, but technological speed should not be confused with creative quality. Its benefits will be greatest when efficiency is combined with strong human standards.

Topic 5 — Human effort and the value of content

Whether the value of creative work lies in its final quality or in the human effort behind it has become increasingly important as generative AI improves. Some argue that consumers should care only about the usefulness or artistic quality of the finished product, whereas others believe that human labour and creative authenticity are themselves valuable. I consider both factors relevant, although their importance varies according to the type of content.

For many functional products, the final result is understandably the main concern. A consumer reading basic instructions, viewing an advertisement or using a simple graphic may have little reason to care how the material was produced, provided that it is accurate and effective. Generative AI can improve efficiency, reduce costs and smooth over the prose of routine documents without meaningfully reducing their value. In these contexts, insisting that every creator do the hard yards manually may simply preserve inefficient practices.

Creative and expert content is different because audiences often value the person behind the work. Readers may buy a book not merely for information but for an author's judgement, experience and individual voice. Similarly, followers may support a content creator because they want access to human expertise rather than machine-generated content. If extensive AI use is concealed, the final product may violate consumer expectations even when its surface quality is acceptable. This is why transparency about the extent of AI use can matter.

In my view, therefore, neither the output nor the process should be treated as universally decisive. Functional content can reasonably be judged mainly by results, while art, commentary and expert work often derive part of their value from human authorship. Consumers should ultimately be given enough information to decide which aspect matters to them.

In conclusion, AI has not made human effort irrelevant; instead, it has made the origin of creative work another characteristic that informed consumers may choose to value.
