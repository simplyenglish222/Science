GRAMMAR DEPARTMENT

Как сказать «если бы…»?

В этой статье постоянно обсуждается альтернативное прошлое: событие не произошло, но мы рассуждаем, что произошло бы при другом исходе. Основная модель — Third Conditional.
IF + HAD + V3 → WOULD / MIGHT / COULD HAVE + V3
would have — предполагаемый результат; might have — возможный результат; could have — возможность / потенциальная способность. В вопросах часть с if часто опускается, потому что гипотетическая ситуация уже понятна из контекста.
If the Soviets had won the space race, they might have established a permanent lunar base.
Если бы СССР выиграл космическую гонку, он, возможно, создал бы постоянную лунную базу.
Нереальное прошлое: if + had + V3. Might have + V3 показывает возможный, но не гарантированный результат.
If they had landed first, how would it have changed the Soviet Union as a whole?
Если бы они высадились первыми, как это изменило бы Советский Союз в целом?
Вопрос о нереальном прошлом: would + subject + have + V3?
Would they still have proclaimed the Moon ‘for all mankind’?
Провозгласили бы они всё равно Луну «для всего человечества»?
Риторический / аналитический вопрос о гипотетическом прошлом. Условие понятно из контекста и поэтому не повторяется.
What might their first words on the Moon have been?
Какими могли бы быть их первые слова на Луне?
Might have been = возможный вариант прошлого: «могли бы быть».
Do you think a successful lunar landing would have prevented the eventual collapse of the Soviet Union?
Как вы думаете, предотвратила бы успешная посадка на Луну последующий распад Советского Союза?
Would have prevented = предполагаемый результат альтернативного прошлого.
Would the USA have tried to one-up the Soviet Union by attempting to go to Mars?
Попытались бы США превзойти СССР, отправившись на Марс?
Ещё один вопрос без if-clause: альтернативное условие уже задано всей темой статьи.
How would modern space exploration be different if the Soviets had been first on the Moon?
Как отличалось бы современное освоение космоса, если бы СССР первым оказался на Луне?
Смешанный тип: условие относится к прошлому (had been), а результат — к настоящему (would ... be different).
Apollo might not have succeeded if NASA had not invested in integrated circuits.
Apollo, возможно, не добился бы успеха, если бы NASA не инвестировало в интегральные схемы.
Отрицательный вариант third conditional: might not have + V3 / if + had not + V3.
I dare say ≠ I dare to say.
I dare say — устойчивое вводное выражение со значением «полагаю», «смею предположить», часто без идеи реальной смелости: I dare say he’s right. I dare to say буквально означает «я осмеливаюсь это сказать» и подчёркивает, что вы решаетесь произнести нечто рискованное или спорное. Поэтому в статье естественно именно I dare say.

INTERVIEW WITH

Dr Christopher Riley

Writer and filmmaker specialising in science, space, engineering and history. He has worked on documentaries including In The Shadow Of The Moon, First Orbit and Neil Armstrong – First Man On The Moon.

Article in brief

This interview explores an alternate history in which the Soviet Union reaches the Moon first. Riley argues that a Soviet victory might have produced a permanent lunar base, but later political and economic decline could still have undermined it.

The interview also explains why the United States ultimately succeeded. Soviet rockets were powerful, yet the clincher was American computing power: investment in integrated circuits enabled small guidance computers and precise landings.

Finally, Riley asks whether a different result might have sent the space race farther. The United States could have tried to one-up the USSR by going to Mars, perhaps pushing the frontier of human footprints beyond Earth orbit much earlier.

PART 1 · A SOVIET VICTORY AND THE SPUTNIK EFFECT

If the Soviet Union had won the space race, Riley thinks it might have developed a permanent lunar base rather than making only brief visits. However, the later break-up of the Soviet Union and massive underinvestment could still have ended the project. Paradoxically, early Soviet success also strengthened the United States: the Sputnik effect encouraged investment in science, universities and computing, creating technological spin-offs whose influence extended far beyond Apollo.

What if the Soviets had won the space race?

I think they would have perhaps established some kind of permanent lunar base in the way they colonised Earth orbit in the Seventies and Eighties. It might have been that they continued to run with a presence on the Moon instead of just sort of going there for a few days and coming back and then never returning, as essentially what has happened now.

However, you’ve got to imprint upon the effect that the break-up of the Soviet Union had on the space programme. That really caused a massive underinvestment, which might have ultimately led to any lunar base being abandoned – and we’d be back where we are today.

Did the successful launch of the Soviet Union’s Sputnik 1 in 1957, the first man-made satellite in space, inspire America to reach for the Moon?

Oh yes, undoubtedly. The “Sputnik effect”, as it’s called, was a significant player in ensuring that Apollo succeeded. President Eisenhower commissioned the Saturn V rocket and he boosted brainpower by investing in universities.

I think Apollo made America smarter for that period – and the legacy of that was, of course, not just to win the Moon race but the spin-offs that happened. Not least the micro-computing processing revolution and ultimately the Internet, of which the early DARPA structures were the forerunner, as they were all wrapped up in the Cold War investments the government had made.

We’ve got our modern society to be thankful for because of that initiative, that “Sputnik effect”. We’re still living off that. It was profound, what Eisenhower did.

PART 2 · WHEN AMERICA TOOK THE LEAD

Riley identifies Apollo 8 as the decisive turning point. Sending astronauts around the Moon on such an early Saturn V flight was very audacious and represented a gamble they managed to pull off. The Soviet N1 failures were a colossal setback, but Riley says the clincher was computing. NASA’s investment in integrated circuits made accurate guidance possible; powerful Soviet rockets alone could not guarantee a landing.

When was the moment that the United States took the lead in the space race?

The Zonds were racing around the Moon unmanned in 1968, so I think you have to point to Apollo 8 in December 1968, which was this very audacious and perhaps even somewhat reckless mission to pull off.

Apollo 8 was previously just an Earth orbit mission, but they instead went straight around the Moon on the first Saturn V launch, which was a very, very brave thing to do. Ultimately that bravery, that gamble that they somehow managed to pull off, was the turning point without a doubt.

Was there any other major turning point that happened during the space race?

The N1 disaster – the Soviet Moon rocket that failed repeatedly – was obviously a colossal setback. But it wasn’t just about booster technology; the Russians easily matched the Saturn V, and they were ahead in booster lift for many years. But the clincher was the computing power; that is where the Russians were really falling short.

How far behind were the Russians in terms of their computing power?

While the Russians might have been able to orbit the Moon, it was a far cry from landing on it. The thing that really clinched the success of Apollo, in no uncertain terms, was their computing power.

The fact that NASA had invested significant amounts of money in the manufacturing of integrated circuits in order to create the micro-computers that were light and small enough to be able to fly on these Apollo spacecraft, and then make these precise landings on the Moon, was decisive. The Russians, as far as I’m aware, didn’t really have that sort of micro-processing capability in those days. Their systems probably wouldn’t have allowed them to really make a successful landing. It wasn’t impossible, but it was quite unlikely.

PART 3 · RISK, PRESTIGE AND A POSSIBLE SOVIET LANDING

The Soviet programme may have relied more heavily on pilot skill, luck and very quick firsts. Riley even suggests Soviet pilots might have tried to wing it manually if computers were insufficient. Had they landed first, the prestige would have been enormous: a returning cosmonaut could have been lauded around the world. Alexei Leonov is Riley’s likely first Moon walker because he had already had to contend with serious difficulties during the first spacewalk.

Did the Soviets realise this?

I think they were just sort of gambling on the judgement of their pilots and hoping they could pull it off without this computing power. The Russian approach to spaceflight in the Sixties, both robotic and human, was a little bit of fingers firmly crossed behind your back as they launched.

Everybody needed an element of luck; luck goes hand in hand with skill and engineering when it comes to spaceflight, of course. But the Russians relied on luck a bit more, and the reason I say that is because they essentially ran for all these very quick firsts in human spaceflight in the Sixties.

For example, they were the first to put three people in a capsule, and they only did that by depriving them of their pressure suits so they could squeeze them into a two-man capsule. Things like that were clearly a bit reckless with the way they went forward.

While they probably were aware that their computing power was inferior to the Americans, I think they just thought they’d wing it and their pilots would hopefully be able to pull a lunar landing off just manually.

If they had landed first, how would it have changed the Soviet Union as a whole?

Well, you’ve got to look at how they reacted to Gagarin returning in April 1961 and Valentina Tereshkova, the first woman in space, in June 1963, and the other heroes of spaceflight that placed Russia so high on the world stage.

I think a successful returning lunar cosmonaut would have been celebrated and lauded around the world in exactly the same way. If you look at the “Giant Step” tour that the crew of Apollo 11 went on in the summer of 1969 when they got back – 40 countries in 30 days or something like that – touring the world with millions of people coming out on the street and giving these ticker-tape parades wherever they went, you can imagine that absolutely would have happened to the Russians as well.

Whether it would have had a material change on the course of Russian history and how their society changed in the Eighties and Nineties, I don’t know. It would have been great when it happened in the Sixties, but perhaps it wouldn’t have made a big difference in the grand scheme of things.

Which Soviet cosmonaut do you think might have taken the first steps on the Moon?

Alexei Leonov’s name often comes up as the first Moon walker, having done the first spacewalk in March 1965 and contended with those difficulties and survived the mission. I think he likes to think he would have been as well from his writings and interviews since – and I dare say he’s right.

Would they still have proclaimed the Moon ‘for all mankind’ as the Americans did?

If you listen to Khrushchev’s speeches at the time, they were all about how Gagarin’s flight was for everybody. The whole point was it was a gift to the world and it was Russia’s great gift to human history, so they would have, I’m sure, done the same thing on the Moon.

Whether they’d have taken a UN flag, which was proposed initially for the Americans to fly rather than the stars and stripes, or whether they’d have planted their own hammer and sickle, I don’t know. I suspect they would have planted their own flag, but their speeches and plaques that they unveiled I’m sure would have had the same sentiments as Gagarin’s flight.

PART 4 · WORDS, PROPAGANDA AND THE FATE OF THE USSR

A Soviet lunar message would probably have been carefully prepared by the central government, unlike Armstrong’s relatively independent choice of words. The USSR might have presented the achievement as a gift to the world, while still using its own flag, speeches and plaques. Yet Riley doubts that even a successful lunar landing would have prevented the eventual collapse of the Soviet Union: prestige cannot necessarily reverse deeper political and economic forces.

What might their first words on the Moon have been?

Well, Neil Armstrong was given complete freedom, as were all of the previous crews of Apollo 8. They decided what they would read or speak, and no one intervened. In fact, while Armstrong had obviously given it a lot of thought, he had a number of options from what his mother told me last year and he made his final decision as to what was going to be said when he was going down the ladder.

I think with the Russians, knowing a bit about how their society worked at the time, it would have been very carefully written. There’s a speech that Gagarin makes before he climbs in the rocket on the first spaceflight in April 1961 and it’s beautifully and poetically sculpted in terms of its message to the world, and it was completely written for him by the central government. I think it would have been a similar sort of speech for the first lunar cosmonaut.

Do you think that a successful lunar landing, broadcast to the world, would have prevented the eventual collapse of the Soviet Union?

No, I don’t. If you look at what it did to America, they won this race and very quickly the country got sick of spending money, and within a few missions after Apollo 11 the programme was cancelled.

The political direction afterwards, both in positive and negative terms, was not really influenced by the success of Apollo, perhaps sadly. So I suspect in Russia it would have been exactly the same.

They would have had this time that they carried on running their bases, maybe on the Moon as we’ve talked about, certainly building a space station in Earth orbit, until effectively politics and perhaps society and the rest of the world overwhelmed them.

PART 5 · MARS AND A DIFFERENT MODERN SPACE AGE

A Soviet lunar victory might have tempted the United States to try to one-up its rival by going to Mars. Riley imagines the Cold War potentially sabre-rattling its way farther into the Solar System, although a Mars mission would have required resources far beyond Apollo. In the most radical version of this history, humanity might have skipped the space-station stage. Because the ISS was conceived and built in connection with the Shuttle era, another strategic path might instead have kept pushing the frontier of human footprints towards the Moon and Mars.

Would the USA have tried to one-up the Soviet Union by attempting to go to Mars?

It’s nice to imagine that the race could have hurdled us down the Solar System to further away, and there’s some sort of validity in that if the Cold War had continued to sabre-rattle its way into the Nineties. Would America have gone even further to prove a point? It’s possible.

Remember Spiro Agnew, the vice president at the time Apollo 11 left for the Moon, said they were going to be on Mars by 1980. So there were plenty of plans. Von Braun’s bottom drawer had loads of concepts in it for adapting and modifying Apollo configurations to send them further.

It’s a lovely thought to imagine that with Apollo hardware, you could have actually had a human footprint on Mars by now.

Would they have succeeded?

I don’t know. I mean, it took four million human years to put those 12 Americans on the Moon – the work of 400,000 people for a decade. I think you could have multiplied that by 100, maybe 1,000, to land on Mars. It would have been very difficult to do, and it still remains so.

How would modern space exploration be different if the Soviets had been first on the Moon?

If – and this is an enormous if, and not one I think likely – the Russians had got to the Moon first and the Americans had gone to Mars, I think we would have skipped the space-station stage, as it were.

The International Space Station was largely conceived and built to justify the Space Shuttle, so we probably wouldn’t have gone down that route. We would have just been pushing the frontier of human footprints across the Solar System, on and on.

I think if we’d become as advanced as we are now, and then changed this mindset from racing to collaborating as a community, we’d again be looking at a sort of equivalent to the space station – a laboratory, but somewhere on the Moon or Mars instead, rather than in Earth orbit.

It would have ultimately been a completely different picture from the years of shuttle flights and space stations that we’ve lived through instead.

Real timeline

1957

First artificial satellite in space

To the shock of the United States and the wider world, the Soviet Union successfully launches Sputnik 1, an unmanned artificial satellite, into space.

31 January 1958

Explorer 1

America strikes back with Explorer 1. The satellite then discovers the Van Allen radiation belts that surround Earth.

2 September 1958

Eisenhower boosts brainpower

To prevent the US falling behind the Soviet Union, President Eisenhower introduces the National Defense Education Act to increase education funding.

12 April 1961

First human in space

The Soviets get another first: Yuri Gagarin becomes the first human in space, weeks before the US launches Alan Shepard.

1968

Apollo 8

NASA must decide whether to send Apollo 8 on a manned or an unmanned flight around the Moon.

21 December 1968

Manned Apollo 8 launches

The three-man crew of Apollo 8 successfully launches and orbits the Moon, paving the way for a manned lunar landing.

21 February 1969

The Soviets fall behind

The Soviet N1 rocket explodes on its first launch and later fails further tests, seriously damaging the lunar programme.

20 July 1969

A giant leap for mankind

Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin become the first humans to set foot on the Moon on Apollo 11, winning the race for the US.

1980s

Space stations

Both the United States and Soviet Union increasingly focus their efforts on space stations and missions in Earth orbit.

2013

International Space Station

The US, Russia and other nations continue to collaborate in Earth orbit, while no humans have ventured farther since 1972.

Alternate timeline

21 December 1968

Unmanned Apollo 8 launches

The US launches Apollo 8 as an unmanned mission, delaying an eventual manned lunar landing and allowing the Soviets to catch up.

1969

Soviets aim for the Moon

Buoyed by stagnation in the American lunar programme, the Soviets prepare to send humans to the Moon after a successful N1 test.

July 1969

Soviet landing

While the US debates the future of Apollo, the Soviets stun the world by sending Alexei Leonov on a daring mission to the lunar surface.

1970

America shoots for Mars

Still reeling from the Soviet space-race victory, America attempts to one-up the Soviets and announces its intention to land humans on Mars.

2013

Space colonisation

After decades of exploration, the US and Russia collaborate on human exploration endeavours throughout the Solar System.
